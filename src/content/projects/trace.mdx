TRACE is my bachelor's thesis at Babeș-Bolyai University, supervised by
Prof. dr. Septimiu Crivei. The question behind it: if an image is going to be
passed around, how can you prove later that it is the one you published, and
that nobody changed it on the way?

A signature in the file's metadata doesn't answer that, because metadata is the
first thing an edit throws away. So TRACE writes the signature into the image
itself. You sign with your private key, and the signature is hidden in the
picture's frequency domain, too small a change for the eye to see. Anyone with
your public key can later read it back out and check it against the pixels.

## How it works

The image's blue channel goes through a one-level Haar wavelet transform. The
low-frequency part that comes out is split into small blocks, each block goes
through a discrete cosine transform, and one bit of the payload is written into
a mid-frequency coefficient of each block. Mid-frequency is the compromise: low
frequencies would show, high frequencies are the first thing compression
throws away.

The payload carries a small header with the block size, the signature length,
the 256-byte RSA-PSS signature itself, the message, and a terminator. Because
the block size is in the header, verifying needs nothing but the image and the
public key.

## What I found

The first version failed more often than it should have. In the benchmark,
114 of 396 valid cases came back with an invalid signature, even though nothing
had touched the image. The cause turned out to be the wavelet transform: when an
image's sides aren't a multiple of the block grid, the transform pads the edges,
and that padding shifted the embedded bits just enough to break the signature.

My fix, which the thesis calls Resonance Optimization, crops the image to a
multiple of twice the block size before the transform, so there is no padding
to go wrong. With it, those 114 failures went to 0: every case where the
message fit in the image produced a valid signature, 396 out of 396, against
282 out of 396 without it. It also made embedding faster, because the fallback
that used to retry around the margins was no longer needed: 0.21 s per image on
average instead of 0.36 s, and 0.73 s instead of 2.67 s in the slowest case.
Average image quality stayed around 35 dB PSNR either way.

The benchmark behind those numbers ran 15 synthetic images across 17 block
sizes, with and without the optimization, three times each: 1,530 runs in all.
The other failures in it are images too small to carry the payload at large
block sizes, and those fail the same way in both modes.

## Choices I'd defend

- **No SVD.** Many watermarking papers use singular value decomposition, but it
  has a known flaw where a forged watermark can still pass as the owner's. I
  left it out on purpose.
- **RSA-2048 over elliptic curves.** It's what web PKI already speaks, so the
  keys work with existing tools.
- **No database at all.** The server keeps nothing between requests, which
  makes privacy a property of the design rather than a promise.

## Limits

It works on lossless images only: PNG and BMP. JPEG compression destroys the
payload, and so does cropping, rotating or scaling a signed image. The
signature and header need roughly 2,128 blocks, which rules out very small
images.

I used AI assistants for parts of the code and the drafting, as the thesis
declares. The design, the algorithm, and the verification are mine.
